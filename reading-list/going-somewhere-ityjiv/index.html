Going Somewhere

simone.org · 2026年10月22日

Life, regardless of location. Chicago, ciao and see you soon.

“Who talks to somebody that they don't know freely, honestly, at six o'clock in the morning?”

The woman driving me home from O'Hare asks it three quarters of the way in. By then the ride has proven her point. She hates airport pickups. “But I do it anyway. I do it, man, for the conversation.”

It is five thirty in the morning and I have fifteen hours of flying behind me. I ordered the Comfort tier because it cost a couple dollars more. You can ask for a quiet ride. The car is newer and it shows up faster. Why not.

My name drops a syllable in America, and I am the one who dropped it. Sim. Like The Sims, like a SIM card. Simone with no photo attached sets up drivers and workers at cafés to expect a lady. Nina Simone here, Simone de Beauvoir there. I'd feel honored with either. She calls me Sim as I open the car's door.

She is a barber who also drives, or a driver who also barbers, depending on the morning. Ten minutes in, she delivers this line: “I'm a recovering addict. Twenty two years. They will never know, because they expect the recovering addict to be looking beat up.”

Back seats stocked: colorful little bags for kids, clothes for whoever can use them. She feeds people most weeks, the ones I often walk past. “I know they name. I will hug them. It could have been me.” She says it as if it's mathematical.

Last Black Friday she and one niece bought another niece a stove and had it delivered. The same girl's father will not buy her a washing machine. “This is your seed you planted, and you're not nurturing it. I don't care how old she is. Everybody needs somebody at some time.” She owns two cars. One sits in a struggling woman's driveway, financed in her name, while the finance company calls her phone. “I'm sidestepping somebody who got it in the car place to give it to somebody who need it.”

When a baby arrives in the family, she says “Welcome to this cruel world. I don't really want to say welcome, but you're here and you gotta thrive. You gotta grow.” She cries at every birth. Six grandchildren so far.

Some mornings her fares are kids headed to school, and she runs the interview. What year are you in. Whatcha gonna do when you get out. Where you plan on being. “This probably some conversation they mama ain't had with them.” Some of them tell her it was the best ride they ever had. She has a reason for asking. “You don't never know who on the edge.”

One night she pulls up to a dark house in some suburb. A kid gets in with his hood up, drawn into the corner of the seat, dressed for disappearing. She turns around before she drives.

“Why are you bagging up in this car? You almost got killed.” He says he is going to football practice. She asks whose name is on the app. His mother's. “You don't have to bag up in the Uber. It's paid for. Your mother called for you.” Then the part he will remember. “You was about to go to hell now. You want to go to the NFL, do it properly. Don't put yourself in danger.”

She read him in one glance. A boy so scared of a stranger's car that he made himself look like the danger. The fear was the costume. She took it off him and drove him to practice.

Then she reads me. “If you don't act scared, they ain't gonna try you.” She glances at my ink and my metal. “You got the piercings. You got that big smile. Ain't nobody gonna just run up on you.” Smiling is public equipment for me. On my own, my face settles to neutral. Elevator, tank top, shades, deep in my own head, and strangers push the button to open the door faster. When I catch it, I send the big smile ahead of me. Walking home at four in the morning in Uptown, I did not catch it. Old fears woke up instead. Big city, late hour, watch yourself, Sim. A woman ahead glanced back at my tank top, the huge blackwork, the piercings, the shades on at night. She picked up her pace. I got as far as “oh, fuck no, I'm the one that people…” and Vicky finished the sentence for me. “You the one that they scared!”

We both burst out laughing.

She has a rule about herself. “If everybody was like me, it'll be a terrible place.” I told her I say that all the fucking time, and I had the receipt. Two weeks earlier a friend bolted mid evening because another friend and I were connecting a bit too much. He read himself as the third wheel. Brother, you're not the third wheel. This is just how we are. If everybody was like us, the world would be a fucking hell. Different lives, same sentences. It kept happening. Neither of us needed anything from the other, so there was nothing to perform.

Vicky was born and raised here. “Been a lot of places, but ain't no place like the Chi.” I have twelve years in, fifteen depending on how you count. I will be tied to this city for the rest of my life. Djo already wrote the song for it, End of Beginning. You take the man out of the city, not the city out the man. Rome stayed in me the whole time here. Chicago is already doing the same in Italy.

“It's a good place. It's a judgmental place. It's a prejudice place.” She's right. Chicago sorts people by map, everybody who lives here knows it. Her car is a shared room. An immigrant from Uptown and a grandmother from the South Side, on God, addiction, stoves, and fear.

Near my street, the money comes up on its own. “This is why I call it the land of milk and honey. I'm out here driving a $53,000 credit to have a conversation with you. At the end of the day this ain't nothing but pennies on the dollar. At the end of the year, Uncle Sam and all the taxes.” I paid thirty two bucks for this ride. “They gave me like sixteen. Half the money. Plus the tip, though. When it comes.”

The app priced the miles and kept half. Everything else in that car was off the books. The Comfort tier sold me a thermostat and the option of silence. The comfort I got was of a different kind. Sixteen dollars, before gas and before Uncle Sam. The tip field asked for a number.

My grazie and my ciao are the signature. Twelve years in this country and I never dropped either one. The accent grants them, and people know they're genuine. She hands back a blessing. I point at the corner and tell her she can stop right here, no need to turn around. But she is already making the turn.

“It don't matter, I'm taking you home. Because I don't know where I'm going after this. But I'm going somewhere.”

Life, regardless of location. Chicago, ciao e a presto.
