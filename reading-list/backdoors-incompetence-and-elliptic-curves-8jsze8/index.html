Backdoors, Incompetence, and Elliptic Curves

johndcook.com · 2026年10月21日

The NIST elliptic curves have been in the news twice lately. A few days ago Anthropic posted a paper on the NIST curve seeds that I wrote about here. Now the curves are in the news again, this time over broken certificates.

My post a few days ago was about the possibility that the NSA had added a backdoor to the NIST curves. Anthropic researchers showed that this wasn’t the case. The NSA had not been able to defend how the curve seeds were chosen because they forgot, but researchers from Anthropic were able to reverse engineer the source of the seeds.

So apparently the NIST curves do not have a backdoor, but as with all encryption, the Achilles heel is human error. eMudrha was using the NIST curve P-384  with extremely small keys: 4, 56, 115, 155, 156, 230, and 237. To understand why this is a problem, I’ll briefly describe how elliptic curve cryptography works.

P-384 is an elliptic curve designed by NIST, with input from the NSA, for use in cryptography. The curve has on the order of 2384 points [1] because the base field is the integers mod

p = 2384 − 2128 − 296 + 232 − 1,

a 384-bit prime number. Given a basepoint b on the curve and an exponent e, it is practically impossible to recover e from be. That’s the discrete logarithm problem [2], the basis for the security of elliptic curve cryptography. If and when cryptographically relevant quantum computers arrive, the discrete logarithm problem will be solvable and elliptic curve cryptography will be broken.

Until the advent of quantum computing, elliptic curve cryptography is presumably safe, unless someone does something negligent, such as using a tiny value of e like 4.

If e is chosen at random from a group with on the order of 2384 elements, you cannot recover e from be without brute force. But apparently someone said

Let’s see. Is e = 1? Is it 2? Is it 3? Is it 4? Why yes: e = 4.

Ordinarily this would be hopeless. You can’t just try 2384 exponents. But someone thought to test whether the keys were horrifically bad, and they were right.

 

[1] I don’t know whether the exact number of points on the curve is known. It’s the number of points on an elliptic curve is not simple to calculate, but it is easy to estimate. It’s roughly the size of the base field, which is p.

Update: There are exactly 39402006196394479212279040100143613805079739270465446667946905279627659399113263569398956308152294913554433653942643 points on the P384 curve. (NIST SP 800-186, §3.2.1.4⁠)

[2] The exponentiation here is not over the integers, or over the integers mod p. It’s over the group structure of the elliptic curve, and that is what makes it hard to compute the discrete logarithm.
