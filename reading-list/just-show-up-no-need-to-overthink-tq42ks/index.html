Just show up, no need to overthink

Yihan文字园艺 · 2026年9月27日

At 6:55pm last Friday, standing close to Albemarle Street, I had to make a decision. Should I take the next Tube home, or head to the Royal Institution (RI) as planned to attend physicist Carlo Rovelli’s discourse?

It was an embarrassing moment. I had invited Leyi to join me for the talk, yet I couldn’t find the tickets! And when I eventually did, I realised that the ticket was for the online stream, not the in-person event, which had already sold out when I booked. Apparently, I had completely forgotten about that!

I decided to go ahead anyway. In my cookbook, there’s always a chance if one shows up. Leyi was reluctant, but agreed to follow suit.

We saw a long queue, joined it, and quickly got our turn. Unsurprisingly, they couldn’t find our names! They checked the guest list and still couldn’t find us. I had to admit that we didn’t actually have the right tickets, even though both of us are RI members.

Surprisingly, we weren’t turned away. Instead, we were directed to reception, where they kindly put our names on the waiting list. Within 10 minutes, we were allowed in!

Once we were seated, I shrugged and told Leyi, “Just show up. That’s my philosophy!”

The simple rule is: you may or may not get in if you show up. But you definitely won’t get in if you don’t.

This rule has been etched in my heart through many, many real-world experiences.

Allow me to be nostalgic again and share a story from my past life.

I once took a PhD course from the business school on empirical research in investment. I was interested because the syllabus included two lectures, out of 13, on behavioural finance, which I thought might be helpful for my thesis in behavioural economics.

I had to write to Prof Hwang to apply, as the course was not offered from my school. He asked for my background and checked which courses I had taken, then rejected my request – I had none of the pre-requisites he specified – doctor-level classes in finance theory, economics, and econometrics.

I pleaded with him: let me attend the first class, and judge for yourself if I qualify.

He agreed.

His opening lecture was divine, and I still remember him telling us about his wife, who had left her career as a senior accountant, started medical school at 40, and was then practicing in Singapore and was immensely proud of her work.

I was totally captivated and asked tons of questions, trying to demonstrate that I actually knew a thing or two about finance and investment! And apparently I managed to fool him, because he invited me to take the class.

Yet, to my great disappointment, the course was not a seminar at all!

I thought he would be doing lectures weekly, but instead, the five of us were required to present one piece of research every week. The presentation requirements look soft and simple enough, but to explain in your own words why a research question is interesting and what it contributes, you have to be deeply immersed in the literature.

Basically, I was being forced to play professor and teach a subject I knew almost nothing about.

At that point, I was ready to quit. I thought I was good enough to understand Prof Hwang’s lecture, but I definitely was far from good enough to teach! But how could I quit after taking so much trouble to register for it？

So I decided to take it, even if I might fail in the end.

And I did suffer for it. I remember going to bed at 3am before almost every class, which started at 9am, because I had to read so, so much in an unfamiliar field. Typing out the full formulas alone was a huge challenge, let alone telling a coherent story about the research.

But in the end, I did it.

And I got the highest mark.

I’m forever grateful for Prof Hwang for taking the risk of accepting me into the class. He gave me the chance to push myself to my utmost limits. More importantly, the experience made me internalise just how important research motivation is, and helped me develop a taste for identifying what makes a research question genuinely interesting.

Below is one of the first presentation I gave.

Thank God I showed up! Showing up is the beginning of all possibilities.

Evidence on the Characteristics of Cross Sectional Variation in Stock Returns

Journal of Finance, vol. LII, No.1 1997

Kent Daniel and Sheridan Titman

Presented by: Yihan Xu

28 Mar 2018

1. Motivation

Background

After examining five common risk factors that affect the returns of stocks, Fama and French (1992, 1993) propose a three-factor asset pricing model, where the expected returns can be mostly accounted for by market factor, firm size, and book-to-market ratio. In general, firms that are small or high in high-to-market ratio have higher expected returns, adjusting for the market beta.

Although scholars agree on the explaining power of firm size and book-to-market ratio in modelling expected returns, they disagree on why such high discount rate is assigned to small and high-to-market firms.

Before Daniel and Titman, scholars regard the excess returns as compensation for bearing certain undiversifiable risk factors:

Risk factors Scholars Mechanisms
Systematic risk: distress risk Fama & French (1993) more sensitive to financial distress/ term structure shifts
Noisy trader risk: simple&naive Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny (1994) over-optimistic to growth firms; over-pessimistic to value firms

In other words, Fama and French (1993) see firm size and book-to-market as proxies for certain unobserved undiversifiable risk factors which induce cross-sectional variation in expected returns.

Research question

This idea is challenged by Daniel and Titman. They contend that the characteristics themselves could directly affect the cross-sectional variation in stock returns.

They want to empirically test whether the cross-sectional variation in expected returns is directly linked to firm size and book-to-market ratio characteristics or indirectly linked to them through common risk factors that vary with firm size and book-to-market ratio.

I have to admit, at first I didn’t fully understand how Daniel & Titman’s model is different from Fama & French’s, until I came up with an analogy. Let me illustrate the research question using it.

Why it’s interesting?

Suppose a salesman Tom discovers that it’s more profitable to sell life insurance to women than to men. His colleagues all uphold Tom’s conjecture as the life expectancy of women are longer than men, but they have different reasonings for that.

  • John: Women live longer than man, because men engage in more unhealthy behaviours (e.g. smoking & drinking) than women.
  • Ana: Women live longer than men, because it’s wired in the genes.

While John believes that gender is mere a proxy of risk factor for life expectancy, Ana believes that gender itself is a determinant of life expectancy.

What evidence would support John and Ana’s reasonings?

We need to compare men and women’s life expectancy (LE) under different scenarios.

  • John needs the following evidence

      Men (LE) Women (LE)
    Healthy long long
    Unhealthy short short

  • Ana needs the following evidence

      Men （LE) Women (LE)
    Healthy short long
    Unhealthy short long

What are the Implications?

Because of the different reasonings, Tom’s colleagues derive distinct implications for targeting potential insurance buyers.

  • If John’s reasoning is more accurate, he would recommend Tom to sell more life insurance to both men and women who engage in more healthy behaviours. And only when Tom couldn’t tell whether they engage in healthy behaviours or not, should he prioritise in targeting women.
  • If Ana’s reasoning is more accurate, she would recommend Tom to sell more life insurance to women, regardless their healthy behaviours. And if he manages to reach out to women who are undervalued by John because they engage in unhealthy behaviours, then better still – he could earn extra income without incurring additional risk.

Recap - Context of the debate

French and Fama (1993) propose the risk theory. They claim that small and value stocks are inherently riskier due to a loading on an unknown distress factor. Since they are riskier, investors should demand higher risk premia.

Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny (1994) propose the mispricing theory. They claim that investors incorrectly extrapolate past growth rates, causing value stocks to be under-priced (high expected returns), and growth stocks to be over-priced (low expected returns).

Danial and Titman’s (1997) propose the characteristic theory. They claims that the cross-section of stock returns can be explained by the firm characteristics. Firms have higher expected returns due to having certain characteristic, e.g. high B/M ratio.

2. Hypotheses

2.1 Model 1: The Null Hypothesis

Consistent with Fama & French (1993)’s factor model, the returns are generated by the following factor structure, where there is a separate “distress” factor with a positive risk premium:

\[\tilde{r}_{i,t} = E[\tilde{r}_{i,t}] + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \beta_{i,j } \tilde{f}_{j,t} + \theta_{i,t-1} \tilde{f}_{D,t} + \tilde{\epsilon}_{i,t}\]

  • $\epsilon_{i,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{ei}^2)$ $,$ $f_{j,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$

  • $\beta_{i,j}$ is the loading of firm i on factor j
  • $\tilde{f}_{j,t}$ is the return on factor j at time t
  • $\theta_{i,t-1}$ is the factor loading of firm i on distress factor $D$, where B/M proxies for $\theta_{i,t-1}$
  • $\tilde{f}_{D,t}$ is the return on factor $D$ at time t

According to this factor pricing model, expected returns are a linear function of all factor loadings: \(E_{t-1}[\tilde{r}_{i,t}] = r_{f,t} + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \beta_{i,j } \lambda_j + \theta_{i,t-1} \lambda_D\)

When $\lambda_D$ is positive, it means that firms that load on this distress factor earn a positive risk premium. $\theta_{i,t-1}$ also varies with time as firms move in and out of distress.

The empirical evidence in Fama and French (1993) that seem to support the null hypothesis:

  • Stocks in the high book-to-market (distressed) portfolio strongly covary with one another -> hinting the existence of a common distress factor $D$
  • Value stocks have high returns compared with growth stocks. -> $\lambda_D$ is positive

However, Daniel and Titman suggest that there are alternative models which don’t assume the existence of the distress factor while not contradicting the above evidence

2.2 Model 2: A Model with Time Varying Factor Risk Premia

The first alternative hypothesis is a time-invariant, J-factor model that describes the variance-covariance matrix of returns. This model assumes no separate distress factor, and the covariance matrix of returns is stable over time.

\[\tilde{r}_{i,t} = E[\tilde{r}_{i,t}] + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \beta_{i,j } \tilde{f}_{j,t} + \tilde{\epsilon}_{i,t}\]
  • $\epsilon_{i,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{ei}^2)$ $,$ $f_{j,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$

No separate distress factor $\tilde{f}_D$, the remaining $\beta’s$ in this model are constant over time, so that the covariance structure doesn’t change as firms move in and out of stress. Accordingly, the factor structure of expected returns is:

\[E[\tilde{r}_{i,t}] = r_{f,t} + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \beta_{i,j } \lambda_{j,t-1}\]

In Model 2, a factor’s risk premium increases following a string of negative factor realizations. On average, firms load on the distressed factor will have high BM ratio, resulting higher expected returns in the high book-to-market portfolio.

Finally, Daniel and Titman assume that

there is an observable variable $\theta_{i,t}$ (i.e., the book-to-market ratio). $\theta$ obeys a slowly mean-reverting process and the innovations in $\theta$ are negatively correlated with past returns (so that distressed firms have high $\theta$’s). This means that, across firms, $\theta$ should be correlated with the factor loading on the currently distressed factor. Therefore, if a portfolio of the stocks of high $\theta$ firms is assembled, the stocks are likely to be those that have high loadings on factors with (currently) high $\lambda$’s. In other words, the high $\theta$ portfolio is successfully timing the factors.

2.3 Model 3: A Characteristic-Based Pricing Model

The characteristic-based model assumes that high book-to-market stocks realize a return premium that is unrelated to the underlying covariance structure.

As in Model 2, covariances are stationary over time and can be described by a factor structure: \(\tilde{r}_{i,t} = E[\tilde{r}_{i,t}] + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \beta_{i,j } \tilde{f}_{j,t} + \tilde{\epsilon}_{i,t}\)

  • $\epsilon_{i,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{ei}^2)$ $,$ $f_{j,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$

However, unlike previous models, model 3 does not use factor loadings to describe expected returns. Instead, they use an observable, slowly varying firm attribute $\tilde{\theta}_{i,t}$ to describe expected returns.

As in Model 2, the innovations in $\theta$ are negatively correlated with the returns on the stock, but $\theta$ is not directly related to the loadings on the distressed factors.

\[E[\tilde{r}_{i,t}] = a + b_1*\tilde{\theta}_{i,t}\]

2.4 Model Comparison and Testing

Model 1 vs. Models 2 and 3:

Their structure of return standard deviation of a portfolio differs.

  • If a distress factor is assumed as in model 1, then when stocks of a portfolio all simultaneously become distressed, the return standard deviation will increase.
  • If there is no distress factor as assumed in Model 2 and 3, then the return standard deviation should stay approximately constant.

Model 1 and 2 vs. Models 3:

The driving force of high returns differs.

  • Model 1 and 2 are factor model, suggesting that the high expected returns are earned by high factor loading of an unobservable risk factor.

  • Model 3 are characteristic model, suggesting that the high expected returns are earned by all distressed firms, regardless of their factor loadings. Model 3 also implies that a clever investor can take arbitrage, earning the book-to-market return premium without loading on any common risk factors.

3. Research design

3.1 Research questions

Danial and Titman’s (1997) wanted to test whether the excess returns of small and value stocks are driven by exposure to common risk factors or by characteristics alone.

The test is empirically challenging as factor loadings of firm size and BM ratio are indeed highly correlated with those characteristics. Therefore, it’s crucial to isolate the variation in the factor loadings from the variation in size and book-to-market characteristics.

In other words, the purpose of the research design is to verify whether stocks with similar characteristics, but different Fama-French loadings, have similar returns or not. And whether stocks with different characteristics, but similar Fama-French loadings, have different returns or not.

The empirical question is nicely summarised in Danial and Titman’s (1998) working paper.

Suppose there are three stocks: A, B, C.

  • Stock A and stock B have the same B/M ratio
  • Stock A has a higher HML loading $\beta_{HML}$ than stock B.
  • Stock C has a higher B/M ratio than stock B.
  • Stock B and stock C have the same HML loading $\beta_{HML}$.

/Screen Shot 2018-03-23 at 11.51.13 PM.png

  • If factor model 1 and 2 are true, then E(A) > E(B), E(B) = E(C)
  • If characteristic model 3 is true, then E(C) > E(B), E(A) = E(B)

3.2 Research procedure

To test these hypotheses, we need to construct two types of portfolios.

  • Type A: portfolios of stocks that have similar factor loadings but different characteristics.
  • Type B: portfolios of stocks with similar characteristics but different factor loadings.

If it’s the factor loadings that determine expected returns, then A should have equal returns. If it’s the characteristics that determine expected returns, then B should have equal returns.

3.3 Test Portfolios formation

The goal is to sort portfolios so that characteristics are orthogonal to factor loadings.

Imagine we form portfolios based on BM ratio. It’s clear that higher BM portfolios will also have higher HML factor loadings, and we will end up with characteristics and $\beta_s$ which are very highly correlated. If we sort portfolios based on some other variables that forecast the factor loadings, this will also yield almost perfect correlation between characteristics and factor loadings.

What we need is to sort stocks along multiple dimensions. In other words, we need to find value stocks that behave like growth stocks, and vice-versa.

  1. Data source: NYSE/Amex & Nasdaq firms, from 1973-1993

  2. Sorting based on B/M and ME: First, rank stocks by book-to-market ratio (3 groups, 33.3%, 67.7%) at the end of year t - 1. Second, rank stocks by firm size (3 groups, 33.3%, 67.7%) at the end of June of year t. Third, starting in July of year t, sort all NYSE/Amex and Nasdaq stocks into the three book-to-market groups and the three size groups based on these breakpoints.

  3. Calculate past factor loadings as proxy for future factor loadings. If the returns of a stock with a high bm ratio hadn’t covered strongly with the returns of current group of high BM stocks in the past 3 years, then they assume they wouldn’t covary as strongly in the future.

  4. Sorting based on past factor loadings: The firms remain in these portfolios from the beginning of July of year t to the end of June of year t + 1. Each of the individual firms in these nine portfolios is then further sorted into one of five sub-portfolios based on their HML coefficients in the regression (The regression is run between 42 months and 6 months prior to the formation date):

    \[R_{i,j,k} – R_{f} = \alpha + \beta_{HML}*\tilde{R}_{HML} + \beta_{SMB}SMB*\tilde{R}_{HML} + \beta_{Mkt}*(R_{Mkt} - R_f)\]

  5. To determine which model best represents the data, Daniel and Titman construct 45 characteristics-balanced(CB) portfolios, which are long-short or zero net-investment portfolios.

  6. The CB portfolio buys and sells equal amounts of high B/M stocks, so it is characteristics-balanced, but it has a high loading on the HML factor.

  7. If the factor-model is correct, then CB portfolios should have a positive return as they have high HML loadings. If the characteristic model is correct, then CB should have a zero average return.

4.5 Portfolios Sorted by Characteristics and Predicted HML Factor Loadings

What would the factor model predict versus the characteristics model predict for these portfolios?

The factor model would suggest the following:

The L/S characteristic balanced portfolios should have positive expected returns because the portfolios are long the high HML firms and short the low HML firms. Alpha should be zero because the Fama and French 3-factor model will pick up the variation in returns via a higher HML loading.

The characteristic model predicts something different:

The L/S characteristic balanced portfolios should have flat expected returns because the portfolios are formed with similar characteristics (Size and B/M). Alpha could be positive because the 3-factor model may not fully pick up the variation in returns due to characteristics.

4.6 Regression Results for the Characteristic-Balanced Portfolios

/Screen Shot 2018-03-24 at 12.04.16 AM.png

As shown below, the combined portfolio, which is long high HML firms and short low HML firms, while controlling for the characteristics, has a positive and significant 3-factor alpha (only one negative $\alpha$, and three of the nine have t statistics above two).

In addition, the mean return to this portfolio is -0.116% per month, and is not significantly different from zero (t = -0.60).

The evidence supports the characteristics model’s predictions: Controlling for characteristics, factor loadings don’t matter; Controlling for loadings, characteristics do matter.

4.7 Implications

After controlling for characteristics, factor loadings don’t tell us much about expected returns. In other words, if an investor is calculating “HML loadings” as a means to tell them something about future returns, they should stop this practice. Instead, investors should identify the book-to-market ratio of their portfolio, which will be much more predictive of expected returns.

In addition, investors can earn risk-free premia by investing in distressed firms that have high factor loadings.

5. Conclusion

Based on the findings, it seems that Daniel & Titman’s characteristics model beats the Fama-French model, at least for the tested time horizon.

However, since their only used the U.S. sample and the time horizon is limited (20 years), such evidence is not necessarily conclusive. Replications are needed to consolidate the conclusion.

Replications

After Daniel and Titman proposed the characteristics model, many other scholars have sought to replicate the findings in different markets and with different time horizons, as Gharhori, Chan and Faff (2006) reviewed. In sum, the superiority of their model has not been well replicated.

Davis, Fama and French (2000) replicated the same analysis while extending the time horizon from 1929 to 1997.

  • Significant $\alpha$ over Daniel & Titman’s 20-year horizon
  • Insignificant $\alpha$ when extended to the whole period

Daniel, Titman and Wei (2001) responded by replicating the same analysis in Japan with time horizon from 1975 to 1997.

  • $\alpha$ only significant at 10% level for the HML loading sorts
  • $\alpha$ insignificant for the SMB and Market loading sorts

Gharhori, Chan and Faff (2006) further replicated the study in Australia with time horizon from 1989 to 2003.

  • The majority of test portfolios revealed higher returns for higher loadings
  • For the HML loading-based test portfolios, the Fama-French $\alpha$ are insignificant
  • For the characteristic-balanced portfolios, very few of the Fama-French $\alpha$ are significant.

6. My reflections

Many scholars have challenged the Fama-French model since its publication, but few have proposed strong and solid empirical tests to do so. Daniel and Titman (1997)’s attack is particularly strong as they developed an empirical test to compare their model to the Fama-French model. Through multi-step portfolio sorting and covariance structure calculation, they managed to isolate the variation in the risk loadings from the variation in size and book-to-market characteristics.

However, given the high correlation between factor loadings and characteristics, I believe both models are quite useful in practice to approximate stocks’ expected returns.

Another interesting argument is from Cochrane’s (2005, p.79) classic textbook Asset Pricing:

If, for example, expected returns were truly related to size, one could buy many small companies to form a large holding company. It would be a "large" company, and hence pay low average returns to the shareholders, while earning a large average return on its holdings. The managers could enjoy the difference. What ruins this promising idea? The "large" holding company will still behave like a portfolio of small stocks—it will have their high betas. Thus, only if asset returns depend on how you behave, not who you are—on betas rather than characteristics—can a market equilibrium survive such simple repackaging schemes.
