C++ futures part 5: std::future and std::shared_future

devblogs.microsoft.com/oldnewthing · 2026年10月16日

Wrapping up our quick introduction to the C++ futures library, we come to the last two parts: std::future and std::shared_future.

The std::future is the starting point. It is a move-only class that this takes the source and forwards its wait and wait_for methods to the source, and forwards its get method to consume.

Forwarding the get to consume means that you can call get only once.

The idea is that std::future is for the case where there is exactly one consumer of the value. Since it is move-only, you can’t copy it; you can only give it away and transfer the power to retrieve the value to the recipient.

The std::future has a method called shared(). This transfers the source from the future to a shared_future object. The shared_future also forwards its wait and wait_for methods to the source, but instead of forwarding its get to the source::consume, shared_future::get() just calls source::get to get a reference to the value storage.

This is what makes the “shared future” shared: The shared_future is copyable, so you can have multiple people all with their own shared_future that share the same source, and each one can separately wait for the value and retrieve the value as many times as they want.

The downside of shared_future is that you always get a const reference, so if you want to perform non-const operations on the result, you’ll have to make a copy and operate on the copy. And that’s not possible at all if the result is itself a move-only type, since you can’t move out of a const reference.

You can see that at the end of the day, all of the pieces of the futures library are just different ways of manipulating the invisible source. Some components push values into the source, and others pull values out of the source.

It took me a while to realize that the most important part of the whole futures library is something you can’t even see.

Bonus chatter: All the names in the futures library are horrible. I mean, really, “packaged task”?

Here’s my attempt to give the pieces better names:

Old name New name
promise promise_source
packaged_task function_promise_source
async(deferred) make_lazy_promise
async(async) make_thread_promise
future promise
shared_future shared_promise

I admit, these names aren’t all that good either. But I prefer to use the word “promise” to refer to the thing that “promises” to produce a value. And the pattern promise_source / promise makes the relationship between the two much clearer: A promise source is a thing that makes promises. And it avoids the hilarity of a non-async async().
